## THE TWELVE-DAY ALIBI

They arrested him for threatening a couple with an AR-15.

Four senior officers came for him at once — a Chief Deputy, a Major, a Lieutenant, and the Chief himself. The warrant said he'd made the threat "on or before May 8th."

There's a bill of sale. Signed, dated, on file.

He didn't buy that rifle until **May 20th.**

Twelve days after the threat he supposedly made with a gun he did not yet own. Nobody caught it — not the judge who set his bond at $150,000, not the four officers who came in force for a man with no violent record.

## THE LIE HIS OWN LAWYERS FINALLY ADMITTED

A blood-warrant affidavit swore he smelled of alcohol. "Moderate odor." That sentence held him for eleven months on a $100,000 bond. The blood draw came back **0.00.**

Years later, in a federal court filing, the officer's own defense attorneys — not Reardon, *them* — conceded in writing: their client stated, on camera, that he could not smell alcohol.

The Magistrate Judge reviewing the case wrote it himself: the record shows the officer lied. *"That is disturbing,"* the judge wrote. His words, not the plaintiff's.

## THE AFFIDAVIT THAT CONTRADICTS ITSELF

One clerk. Two sworn documents. One year apart.

2024: a letter, on the record, describing exactly what information he gave the DA about Reardon's pending appeal. 2025: a federal affidavit, sworn under oath, stating he never gave the DA that information at all.

A state appellate court later ruled — independently — that the underlying appeal was never even final. Which means the 2025 affidavit wasn't just contradicted by the 2024 letter. It was contradicted by the law itself.

## NINE MOTIONS. THIRTY-TWO SUPREME COURT CASES. ONE MORNING IN JUSTICE COURT.

No law degree. No bar card. No law firm behind him.

Standing alone in Union County Justice Court, filing pro se against a Sheriff, a Board President, and a county government with unlimited outside counsel — he filed nine motions in a single morning, citing thirty-two Supreme Court cases from memory and a stack of printouts.

The judge hearing the motions used profanity from the bench. Reardon didn't. It's on the record, both ways.

Weeks later he'd drive three hours to a federal courthouse to file the full complaint in person — and get arrested at the door for it.

## THE $34,000 THAT VANISHED TWICE

First it was $4,000, gone from a narcotics unit's lockbox at a local bank. The FBI looked. Insufficient evidence to charge anyone.

Then it was $30,000 more — discovered during a change-of-command inventory by the same police chief who was, on paper, one of the lockbox's own authorized signatories.

He was one of only two people who "discovered" the shortage. He was also one of the people who could have caused it. Nobody outside the department investigated that math.

Years later, that same chief would be the man delaying Reardon's jail release, blocking his car from leaving a courthouse, and telling a federal judge that dashcam footage of Reardon's own DUI stop was simply "gone forever."

A mayor's husband — a lawyer whose own practice profited from the narcotics unit's cases — called the missing-money story "old news" to a reporter before it was ever publicly reported. On the record, from a named source.

Small county. Small circle. Same names keep showing up on both sides of the badge.

## 150 MILES FROM HIS CAR

He walked into New Orleans City Hall to do his job — filming, asking questions, exercising a right that's been settled law for a decade. A security guard hit him in the head with a metal flashlight. Split it open. He woke up at Tulane Medical Center.

The guard was never charged. Reardon was.

When the charge finally collapsed months later — dismissed, "law enforcement issue" stamped right on the disposition — nobody drove him back. They released him **150 miles from his car.** No ride. No transport. A six-hour wait for a bus, alone, at night.

## YOU NEED A PHOTO ID TO EXERCISE YOUR CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS

There is no federal law requiring photo ID to enter a federal courthouse and file a civil complaint. He drove three hours to file one in person. Security stopped him at the door. The Police Chief arrived, took a security guard's word for it, and arrested him — for filing paperwork. They towed his car from the federal lot while he was in cuffs.

Three days later he published the whole thing under his own byline. Four days after that, a police captain in the same department forwarded the article to his colleagues with one line: *"Watch the video with us in it. Lol."*
