## THE TWELVE-DAY ALIBI

They arrested him for threatening a couple with an AR-15.

Four senior officers came for him at once — a Chief Deputy, a Major, a Lieutenant, and the Chief himself. The warrant said he'd made the threat "on or before May 8th."

There's a bill of sale. Signed, dated, on file.

He didn't buy that rifle until **May 20th.**

Twelve days after the threat he supposedly made with a gun he did not yet own.

Nobody caught it. Not the judge who set his bond at $150,000. Not the four officers who came in force for a man with no violent record. It sat in a case file for years, waiting for someone to actually read the paperwork.

*This is the case that opens American Injustice — and it's the least surprising thing in it.*

## THE LIE HIS OWN LAWYERS FINALLY ADMITTED

A blood-warrant affidavit swore he smelled of alcohol. "Moderate odor." That single sentence held him for eleven months on a $100,000 bond.

The blood draw came back **0.00.**

Years later, in a federal court filing, the officer's own defense attorneys — not Reardon, *them* — wrote it in black and white: their client stated, on camera, that he could not smell alcohol.

Not a leak. Not a rumor. A sworn court document, filed by the people defending him, conceding the thing he'd been saying all along.

The Magistrate Judge reviewing the case put it plainly in his own written opinion: the record shows the officer lied.

*"That is disturbing,"* the judge wrote. His words, not the plaintiff's.

## THE AFFIDAVIT THAT CONTRADICTS ITSELF

One clerk. Two sworn documents. One year apart.

**2024:** A letter, on the record, describing exactly what information he gave the Galveston DA about Reardon's pending appeal.

**2025:** A federal affidavit, sworn under oath, stating he never gave the DA that information at all.

Same clerk. Opposite claims. Both on paper, both dated, both filed where anyone can pull them.

A state appellate court would later rule — independently, in an unrelated proceeding — that the underlying appeal was never even final. Which means the 2025 affidavit wasn't just contradicted by the 2024 letter.

It was contradicted by the law itself.

## 150 MILES FROM HIS CAR

He walked into New Orleans City Hall to do his job — filming, asking questions, exercising a right that's been settled law for a decade.

A security guard hit him in the head with a metal flashlight. Split it open. He woke up at Tulane Medical Center.

**The guard was never charged.**

**Reardon was.**

Two months later, during an ice storm, they arrested him again — at a Red Cross warming shelter, three parishes away — extradited him back to New Orleans, and held him for days on a bond he could've posted immediately, except they told him he needed a Louisiana driver's license first. He didn't.

When the charge finally collapsed — dismissed, "law enforcement issue" stamped right on the disposition — nobody drove him back.

They released him **150 miles from his car.** No ride. No transport arranged. A six-hour wait for a bus, alone, at night.

## YOU NEED A PHOTO ID TO EXERCISE YOUR CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS

There is no federal law requiring photo ID to enter a federal courthouse and file a civil complaint. None. It's public building. That's the point of public buildings.

He drove three hours to file a 100-page federal lawsuit in person. Security stopped him at the door. Twenty minutes evaporated waiting on someone who never showed. Then the Police Chief arrived, took the security guard's word for it, and arrested him — for filing paperwork.

They towed his car from the federal lot while he was in cuffs.

Three days later, he published the whole thing under his own byline.

Four days after *that*, a police captain in the same department forwarded the article to his colleagues with one line:

*"Watch the video with us in it. Lol."*
