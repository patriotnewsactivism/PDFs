The Twelve-Day Alibi

They arrested him for threatening a couple with an AR-15. Four senior officers came for him at once. The warrant said he made the threat "on or before May 8th."

He didn't buy that rifle until May 20th. Twelve days after a threat made with a gun he did not yet own.

Nobody caught it — not the judge who set his bond at $150,000, not the four officers who came in force for a man with no violent record.

The Lie His Own Lawyers Finally Admitted

A blood-warrant affidavit swore he smelled of alcohol. That sentence held him eleven months on a $100,000 bond. The blood draw came back 0.00.

Years later, the officer's own defense attorneys conceded in a federal filing: their client stated, on camera, he could not smell alcohol.

The Magistrate Judge reviewing the case wrote it himself: "That is disturbing." His words. Not the plaintiff's.

The Affidavit That Contradicts Itself

One clerk. Two sworn documents, one year apart. A 2024 letter proving he gave the DA appeal information. A 2025 federal affidavit swearing under oath that he never did.

A state appellate court later ruled the underlying appeal was never even final — contradicting the affidavit twice over.

Nine Motions. Thirty-Two Supreme Court Cases. One Morning.

No law degree. No bar card. Standing alone in Justice Court against a Sheriff, a Board President, and unlimited county counsel, he filed nine motions in a single morning citing thirty-two Supreme Court cases from memory.

The judge hearing the motions used profanity from the bench. Reardon didn't. It's on the record, both ways.

The $34,000 That Vanished Twice

First $4,000 disappeared from a narcotics unit's lockbox. The FBI found insufficient evidence to charge anyone. Then $30,000 more vanished — discovered by the same police chief who was, on paper, one of the lockbox's own authorized signatories.

A mayor's husband, whose law practice profited from that same narcotics unit's cases, called the missing-money story "old news" — before it was ever publicly reported.

Years later, that same chief would delay Reardon's jail release, block his car from leaving a courthouse, and tell a federal judge that his own DUI-stop footage was simply "gone forever."

150 Miles From His Car

A security guard split his head open with a metal flashlight at New Orleans City Hall. The guard was never charged. Reardon was — and when that charge finally collapsed months later, they released him 150 miles from his car. No ride. No transport. A six-hour wait for a bus, alone, at night.

You Need a Photo ID to Exercise Your Constitutional Rights

There is no federal law requiring photo ID to file a civil complaint at a federal courthouse. He drove three hours to file one in person and was arrested at the door — his car towed from the federal lot while he sat in cuffs.

Four days after he published the story, a police captain forwarded the article to colleagues with one line: "Watch the video with us in it. Lol."

This Is Not a Clean Story. That's Why It's a True One.

American Injustice doesn't ask you to believe in a perfect victim. It asks you to look at the documents — bills of sale, sworn affidavits, court filings, a judge's own written words — and decide for yourself. It also doesn't hide the parts that are harder to defend, because that honesty is what makes the undeniable parts impossible to wave away.

This was seven stories. The book has thirty more like it.
